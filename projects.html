Autocracies & International Diplomatic Cooperation: Collaboration and UN Engagement by Illiberal States

Abstract:Amidst the growing influence of authoritarians in the international sphere, an important question emerges: why do autocracies participate in liberal and democratic international institutions, and how do they do so differently than actors whose values more closely align with liberal IOs’ missions? Recent scholarship indicates that autocrats' more limited domestic constraints impacts their strategic bargaining decisions in cooperative settings, but empirical tests of the implications of these dynamics remain lacking. I develop a theoretical framework of autocratic engagement and cooperation, and I work to explain the rationalist basis for patterns of compliance and dissent of liberal norms by autocrats. Then, I conduct two social networks to analyse co-sponsorship and co-voting behavior of illiberal states. My findings indicate that autocrats strategically cooperate to contest liberal norms in the United Nations and point to the issue areas where autocrats dedicate most of their diplomatic efforts in cooperation. These analyses lay the groundwork for further empirical research into authoritarian contestation and diplomatic foreign policy strategies.

Advancing Authoritarianism? Domestic Political Stability Outcomes of Chinese Foreign Aid in the Developing World

What are the effects of Chinese foreign aid on recipient state regime stability, and how does this relationship account for the consolidation of authoritarianism abroad? Political scholarship has debated the domestic effects of Chinese aid; however, this aid’s effects on domestic political stability are notably understudied, especially in Latin America. To address this gap, I argue that Chinese aid’s effect on political stability is conditioned upon domestic regime and contributes to increased stability in autocracies. I present a novel theoretical framework of domestic incentives for recipient state actors and five potential causal mechanisms, and I provide mixed-methods evidence to identify a positive relationship between Chinese foreign aid and autocratic stability in recipient states. These findings have significant implications for scholars’ and policymakers’ understanding of the long-lasting effects of the Belt and Road Initiative and Beijing’s aid program more broadly.
